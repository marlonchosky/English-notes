# Accusative Verbs vs. Unaccusative Verbs

---

## 1. Accusative Verbs (Transitive Verbs)

An **Accusative Verb** is the formal syntactic term for a traditional **Transitive Verb**. It is named "accusative" because the direct object DP occupies the structural slot that receives **accusative case**.

* **Core Logic:** `Subject (Agent/Doer) → Verb → Object (Patient/Undergoer)`
* **Example:** *The chef **broke** the egg.*
  * **The chef:** The active agent initiating the action.
  * **the egg:** The direct object receiving the action (structurally assigned accusative case).

---

## 2. Unaccusative Verbs (Patient-Subject Intransitives)

An **Unaccusative Verb** is an intransitive verb where the grammatical surface subject does not initiate or control the action, but instead acts semantically as the **Patient** or **Theme** undergoing a spontaneous physical change of state or motion.

* **Core Logic:** `Subject (Patient/Theme) → Verb`
* **Why "Un-accusative"?** The underlying patient entity is promoted to the subject position to satisfy syntactic requirements ($\text{Spec, TP}$), leaving no post-verbal direct object slot to receive accusative case.
* **Example:** *The egg **broke**.*
  * **The egg:** Surface grammatical subject, but semantically the undergoer/patient.

---

## 3. The 3-Way Taxonomy of Unaccusatives & Resultatives

Because the subject of an unaccusative verb is an underlying patient/theme, unaccusatives naturally host **Resultative Secondary Predicates** specifying the final state of the subject. 

They split into three clear structural behaviors:

```text
Unaccusative Verbs (Patient-Subject Intransitives)
│
├── 1. Bare / Standalone Unaccusatives (Complete Intransitive Events)
│      • No secondary predicate needed; event is syntactically & semantically closed.
│      • Examples: "The butter melted." / "The train arrived." / "The window shattered."
│
├── 2. Standalone Unaccusatives + Optional Resultative Adjuncts
│      • The base clause is fully complete on its own.
│      • The secondary predicate is grammatically optional (an Adjunct) that adds an emphatic final boundary.
│      • Examples: "The river froze [solid]." / "The lake water froze [thick]." / "The staging container melted [flat]."
│
└── 3. Complex Unaccusatives + Obligatory Resultative Complements
       • The verb denotes an open trajectory, motion path, or radical transformation that crashes without an endpoint.
       • The secondary predicate is syntactically mandatory (a Complement).
       ├── AP Subject Complement: "The door blew open." / "The gate swung open."
       └── PP Resultative Complement: "The vase broke into pieces." / "The ice melted into water."
```

### Group 1: Bare / Standalone Unaccusatives (Zero Secondary Predicate)
Robust unaccusatives inherently contain a complete event boundary (culmination) within their lexical root:
* *The butter **melted**.*
* *The window **shattered**.*
* *The train **arrived**.*
* **Status:** Fully grammatical and syntactically saturated as bare intransitive clauses.

### Group 2: Standalone Unaccusatives with Optional Resultative Adjuncts
When a bare unaccusative is already complete, an extra adjective phrase can be added to emphasize the absolute final state. Because deleting it leaves a fully grammatical sentence, it functions as a **Resultative Adjunct**:
* *The river **froze** [**solid**].*
  * **Base Clause:** *"The river froze."* (Complete, fully grammatical sentence).
  * **Syntactic Role:** *solid* is an optional **Resultative Adjunct** modifying the subject.
* *The staging container **melted** [**flat**].*
  * **Base Clause:** *"The staging container melted."* (Complete sentence).
  * **Syntactic Role:** *flat* is an optional **Resultative Adjunct**.

### Group 3: Complex Unaccusatives with Obligatory Resultative Complements
Certain unaccusative verbs express an incomplete directional trajectory or denote a radical physical transition that requires a structural delimiter to close the event. Deletion causes the clause to crash or lose its intended bounded sense:

1. **Adjective Phrase (AP) Subject Complements (Motion / State Paths):**
   * *The door **blew** **open**.* (Wind/pressure moves the door; deleting *open* leaves *"The door blew"* ungrammatical/semantically incomplete).
   * *The gate **swung** **open**.* (Without *open*, the culminated event is incomplete).
   * **Syntactic Role:** Obligatory **Resultative Subject Complement**.

2. **Prepositional Phrase (PP) Complements (The "Into" Transition Rule):**
   * *The vase **broke** **into pieces**.*
   * *The ice **melted** **into water**.*
   * **The "Into" Rule:** When an unaccusative denotes radical physical transformation, fragmentation, or phase change, English selects a PP headed by *into*. Because the verb requires this trajectory to mark event culmination, the PP is an obligatory **Prepositional Resultative Complement** (not an optional adjunct).

## 4. Summary Matrix: Complement vs. Adjunct in Unaccusatives

| Sentence | Base Clause Complete? | Secondary Predicate | Status | Syntactic Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *The river froze solid.* | ✅ Yes (*The river froze*) | *solid* (AP) | **Optional** | Resultative Adjunct |
| *The container melted flat.* | ✅ Yes (*The container melted*) | *flat* (AP) | **Optional** | Resultative Adjunct |
| *The door blew open.* | ❌ No (*\*The door blew*) | *open* (AP) | **Obligatory** | Resultative Subject Complement |
| *The gate swung shut.* | ❌ No (*\*The gate swung shut [as an event]*) | *shut* (AP) | **Obligatory** | Resultative Subject Complement |
| *The vase broke into pieces.* | ❌ No (Lacks transformation bound) | *into pieces* (PP) | **Obligatory** | Prepositional Resultative Complement |
