# Complex Transitive Verbs (CTV) vs. Transitive Prepositional Verbs

Both verb classes follow an active surface sequence of:
$$\text{[Subject]} + \text{[Verb]} + \text{[Direct Object DP]} + \text{[Prepositional Phrase PP]}$$

However, their underlying syntactic architecture, argument mapping, and complement types diverge fundamentally.

---

## 1. The Core Functional Split

| Dimension                   | Complex Transitive Verb (CTV) Frame                          | Transitive Prepositional Verb Frame                          |
| :-------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Verb Type**               | Complex Transitive Verb ($\text{CTV}$)                       | Transitive Prepositional / Prepositional Ditransitive        |
| **Core Argument Structure** | $\text{Subj} + \text{V} + \text{DO} + \text{Secondary Predicate / Bound}$ | $\text{Subj} + \text{V} + \text{DO} + \text{Oblique Argument (PP)}$ |
| **PP Function**             | **Adverbial Complement** OR **Object Complement**            | **Prepositional Complement** (Oblique Argument)              |
| **PP Role**                 | Sets an external coordinate/boundary OR resulting object state. | Supplies a required participant/entity slot (Theme, Threat, Co-agent). |
| **Canonical Verbs**         | *put, keep, place, leave, set*                               | *provide ... with, protect ... from, charge ... with*        |

---

## 2. Adverbial Complement vs. Object Complement (Inside CTVs)

Within a Complex Transitive frame, the post-object phrase serves one of two distinct structural duties:

### A. Adverbial Complement (Spatial, Directional, or Bounded Domain)
The complement establishes an **external physical coordinate, path, or bounded perimeter** containing or directing the direct object. It does **not** alter the intrinsic identity, classification, or physical shape of the noun.
* *"They **put** the book **on the table**."*
  * **put:** $\text{CTV}$
  * **the book:** $\text{DO}$
  * **on the table:** Locative Adverbial Complement ($\text{PP}$)
* *"He **kept** the secret **to himself**."*
  * **keep:** $\text{CTV}$
  * **the secret:** $\text{DO}$
  * **to himself:** Adverbial Complement of Bounded Domain ($\text{PP}$)

### B. Object Complement (State, Quality, Role, or Result)
The complement acts as a **secondary predicate** that directly modifies the Direct Object, assigning it an internal property, mental judgment, resulting state, or nominal identity ($DO = State/Identity$).
* *"He painted the door **red**."* (Resultative $\text{AP}$ $\rightarrow$ Object Complement)
* *"They elected him **manager**."* (Identity $\text{NP}$ $\rightarrow$ Object Complement)
* *"I left him **with the bill**."* (Circumstantial Result State $\rightarrow$ Prepositional Object Complement)

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Adverbial Complement:} \quad & \text{DO} \longrightarrow \text{placed at / restricted to External Coordinate} \\
\text{Object Complement:} \quad & \text{DO} \Longrightarrow \text{bears Internal State or Identity}
\end{aligned}$$

---

## 3. Transitive Prepositional Verbs (Prepositional Ditransitives)

A **Transitive Prepositional Verb** selects two internal arguments: a **Direct Object** and an obligatory **Prepositional Complement** introduced by a fixed lexical preposition.

* **Sentence:** *"I **provided** him **with the file**."*
  * **provided:** Transitive Prepositional Verb
  * **him:** Direct Object ($\text{DP}$)
  * **with the file:** Prepositional Complement ($\text{PP}$, Oblique Object)
* **Sentence:** *"The shield **protected** the reactor **from damage**."*
  * **protected:** Transitive Prepositional Verb
  * **the reactor:** Direct Object ($\text{DP}$)
  * **from damage:** Prepositional Complement ($\text{PP}$, Oblique Source/Threat)

> ⚠️ **Syntax Guardrail:** Do not label *with the file* or *from damage* as an "Object Complement" or "Adverbial Complement." The phrase does not locate the direct object in space, nor does it express an adjective-like result state of the object; it introduces a mandatory semantic participant selected directly by the verb root.
