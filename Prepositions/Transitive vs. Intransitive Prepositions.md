# Transitive vs. Intransitive Prepositions ($P^0$)

In modern generative syntax (such as *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* by Huddleston & Pullum), **Prepositions ($P^0$)** are categorized by their complementation requirements, directly mirroring the transitivity system of verbs.

---

## 1. The Functional Split: Transitive vs. Intransitive $P^0$

Just as verbs can be Transitive (*"He hit [DP the ball]"*) or Intransitive (*"He slept"*), prepositions fall into two distinct transitivity classes:

| Transitivity Class           | Definition                                                   | Structural Formula | Example Construction                                      |
| :--------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Transitive Preposition**   | Demands an overt DP object/complement to complete its phrase. | `[PP P° + DP]`     | *in the house*, *on the table*, *from the office*         |
| **Intransitive Preposition** | Functions as a complete spatial/directional $PP$ head without taking a direct $DP$ object. | `[PP P°]`          | *away*, *home*, *indoors*, *abroad*, *downstairs*, *back* |

---

## 2. Why Standalone Locatives are $P^0$ (Not Pure Adverbs)

Traditional grammar classifies words like *away*, *indoors*, or *downstairs* as "Adverbs of Place." Modern generative syntax classifies them as **Intransitive Prepositions ($P^0$)** for three structural reasons:

### A. Prepositional Degree Modifiers
Intransitive prepositions accept degree modifiers exclusive to $PP$ structures (such as *right* or *straight*), which pure Manner Adverbs reject.

* ✅ *He went **right** indoors.* (`indoors` = Intransitive Preposition)
* ✅ *We are **right** away.* (`away` = Intransitive Preposition)
* ❌ *He drove **right** quickly.* (Crash: Manner Adverbs reject *right*)

### B. Ability to Select PP Complements
Intransitive prepositions frequently head a spatial phrase that selects a second Prepositional Phrase as its complement:

* *We are three minutes [PP **away** [PP from the office]].*
  * **Head ($P^0$):** *away*
  * **Complement:** *from the office*

Pure adverbs rarely take prepositional complements in this manner.

### C. The Ellipsis Proof (Transitive Drop)
Consider directional prepositions that alternate between taking an object and standing alone:

1. *He went [PP **out** the door].* (Transitive Preposition taking $DP$ *the door*)
2. *He went [PP **out**].* (Intransitive Preposition — `out` does not change part of speech simply because its object was omitted).

---

## 3. Core Member List of Intransitive Prepositions

Common intransitive prepositions in English include:

* **Spatial / Directional:** *away, home, indoors, outdoors, abroad, overseas, downstairs, upstairs, back, ahead, nearby.*
* **Aspectual / Particle Shift:** *off, on, out, in, down, up* (when standing alone without an overt object).

---

## 4. Phrasal Architecture Comparison

```text
Transitive PP:
     PP
    /  \
  P°    DP
  |     |
 in   the house

Intransitive PP (Bare):
     PP
     |
     P°
     |
   away

Intransitive PP with Measure Specifier & PP Complement:
          PP
        / | \
     Spec P°  PP Complement
      |   |        |
[3 mins] away [from the office]
```

## 💡 System-Design Note for Your Notes

* **Anatomy / Category ($XP$):** In trees, words like *away* head a **Prepositional Phrase ($\text{PP}$)**.
* **Function:** Serves as a Locative/Spatial Complement or Spatial Measure Adjunct depending on whether it is selected by a verb (like copular *be* or motion *walk*).
* **Traditional Mapping:** Older traditional grammars label these "Adverbs of Place/Distance," which is simply the legacy functional term for an **Intransitive Preposition**.
