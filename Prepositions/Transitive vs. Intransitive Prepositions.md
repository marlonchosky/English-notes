# Prepositions: Transitive vs. Intransitive ($P^0$)

In modern generative syntax (such as *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* by Huddleston & Pullum), Prepositions ($P^0$) are categorized by their complementation requirements, directly mirroring the transitivity system of verbs.

---

### 1. The Functional Split: Transitive vs. Intransitive $P^0$

Just as verbs can be Transitive (*He hit [DP the ball]*) or Intransitive (*He slept*), prepositions fall into two distinct transitivity classes based on whether they require an overt noun object:

| Transitivity Class           | Definition                                                   | Structural Formula | Example Construction                            |
| :--------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| **Transitive Preposition**   | Demands an overt DP object/complement to complete its phrase. | `[PP P° + DP]`     | *in the house, on the table, from the office*   |
| **Intransitive Preposition** | Functions as a complete spatial/directional $PP$ head without taking a direct $DP$ object. | `[PP P°]`          | *away, home, indoors, abroad, downstairs, back* |

---

### 2. Why Standalone Locatives are $P^0$ (Not Pure Adverbs)

Traditional grammar classifies words like *away, indoors,* or *downstairs* as "Adverbs of Place." Modern generative syntax classifies them as **Intransitive Prepositions ($P^0$)** for three structural reasons:

1. **Prepositional Degree Modifiers:** Intransitive prepositions accept degree modifiers exclusive to $\text{PP}$ structures (such as *right* or *straight*), which pure manner/time adverbs reject (*right away*, *straight home*).
2. **The Ellipsis Proof (Transitive Drop):** Directional prepositions can alternate between taking an object and standing alone without changing their word class:
   - *He went [PP out the door].* (Transitive Preposition taking a $\text{DP}$ complement)
   - *He went [PP out].* (Intransitive Preposition — *out* does not change part of speech simply because its object was dropped via context).

---

### 3. Core Member List of Intransitive Prepositions

* **Spatial / Directional:** *away, home, indoors, outdoors, abroad, overseas, downstairs, upstairs, back, ahead, nearby.*
* **Aspectual / Particle Shift:** *off, on, out, in, down, up* (when standing alone without an overt object).

---

### 4. Phrasal Architecture Comparison (Framework A Alignment)

Depending on the context, these prepositional phrases integrate cleanly into clause structures where locative verbs act as **Complex Intransitive Verbs (CIVs)** requiring an **Adverbial Complement**:

#### A. Transitive PP Structure
```text
       PP
      /  \
    P°    DP
    |      |
   in  the house
```

#### B. Bare Intransitive PP Structure
```text
       PP
       |
       P°
       |
     away
```

#### C. Complex Measure PP Structure (with Specifier and PP Complement)
When paired with a locative CIV (*are*), this full structure serves as an **Adverbial Complement of Measure / Location**:

```text
         PP (Adverbial Complement)
        /  |  \
     Spec  P°  PP Complement
      |    |        |
   [3 mins] away [from the office]
```

## 💡 System-Design Note for Your Notes

* **Anatomy / Category ($XP$):** In trees, words like *away* head a **Prepositional Phrase ($\text{PP}$)**.
* **Function (Framework A):** When selected by a locative Complex Intransitive Verb (like *are* in *"We are three minutes away from the office"*), the entire $\text{PP}$ structure serves syntactically as an **Adverbial Complement of Measure and Location**.
* **Traditional Mapping:** Older traditional grammars label these words "Adverbs of Place/Distance," but modern generative syntax proves they maintain prepositional phrase architecture ($P^0$).
