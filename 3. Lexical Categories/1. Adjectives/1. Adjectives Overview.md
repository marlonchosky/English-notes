# Adjectives and Adjective Phrases (AP)

An **Adjective** ($A^\circ$) is a lexical word class (Form / Part of Speech) that characterizes, describes, or restricts a nominal entity. When an adjective takes optional degree specifiers or complements, it projects an **Adjective Phrase** ($\text{AP}$).

Adjectives function across two primary environments:
* **Nominal-Internal Modification:** Operating inside a Determiner Phrase ($\text{DP}$) as **Attributive** or **Postpositive** modifiers.
* **Clausal Predication:** Operating at the clause level as primary **Subject Complements** (copular frames) or secondary **Depictive / Resultative Predicates**.

---

## 1. Structural Distribution: Attributive vs. Predicate vs. Postpositive

### A. Attributive Adjectives
Attributive adjectives sit pre-nominally inside the $\text{DP}$, preceding the head noun they restrict:
* *The **interesting** book.*
* *A **resilient** database architecture.*

### B. Predicate Adjectives
Predicate adjectives sit post-verbally and relate back to a noun phrase via a copular or linking verb, functioning as a **Subject Complement**:
* *The book is **interesting**.*
* *The primary cluster remains **operational**.*

### C. Postpositive Adjectives
Postpositive adjectives follow immediately after the noun they modify. In modern English, postpositive positioning is strictly licensed by three structural conditions:

1. **Fixed Idiomatic Phrases and Legal/Official Titles:**
   * Archaic Romance and Norman-French syntactic borrowings preserved in institutional formulas:
   * *attorney **general**, court **martial**, heir **apparent**, poet **laureate**, notary **public**.*

2. **Compound Indefinite Pronouns:**
   * Compound indefinite pronouns (*something, anything, someone, anyone, nobody, nothing*) fuse a determiner with a nominal base into an indivisible lexical head. They reject pre-nominal adjectives, forcing modifiers into the postpositive position:
   * *I need to configure something **important**.* (❌ *\*an important something*).
   * *The audit revealed nothing **suspicious**.* (❌ *\*a suspicious nothing*).

3. **Heavy Adjective Phrases (Adjectives with Extra Phrasal Weight):**
   * When an adjective takes its own post-head complement (a Prepositional Phrase or Infinitival Clause), the resulting $\text{AP}$ becomes "heavy". Placing heavy APs in the pre-nominal position triggers a syntactic crash; they must shift post-nominally:
   * Standard Pre-Nominal: *The **available** rooms.*
   * Postpositive Shift: *The rooms **[AP available for tonight]**.*
   * ❌ *\*The [capable of horizontal scaling] database engine.*
   * ✅ *The database engine **[AP capable of horizontal scaling]**.*

## 2. Master Classification Matrix: Syntactic Function vs. Semantic Role

Syntactic analysis requires separating the structural requirement (**Function: Adjunct vs. Complement**) from the temporal/event meaning (**Semantic Role: Depictive vs. Resultative**):

```text
Classification of Adjectives in Predication
├── 1. By Syntactic Function (Sentence Structure):
│   ├── Adjuncts (Optional secondary predicates; pass the deletion diagnostic)
│   └── Complements (Obligatory arguments; fail the deletion diagnostic)
│
└── 2. By Semantic Role (Event Temporal Orientation):
    ├── Depictive (Describes state DURING the event)
    │   ├── Depictive Adjunct:     "I ate the pizza cold." / "The river glowed blue."
    │   └── Depictive Complement:  "I found the walls yellow." / "She considers him brilliant."
    │
    └── Resultative (Describes state RESULTING FROM the event)
        │
        ├── Resultative Complements (Obligatory Endpoints / Causative Shifts)
        │   ├── Resultative Object Complement (Complex Transitive Frame):
        │   │   • "He hammered the metal flat." (Hammering causes [metal → flat]).
        │   │   • "They painted the wall yellow."
        │   │   • "He licked the plate clean."
        │   │
        │   ├── Resultative Subject Complement (Unaccusative / Copular Endpoints):
        │   │   • "The door blew open." (Without 'open', the result vector is stranded).
        │   │   • "The milk turned sour." (Aspectual/copular frame requiring an AP complement).
        │   │
        │   └── Prepositional Resultative Complement (PP Realization - The "Into" Rule):
        │       • "The vase broke into pieces."
        │       • "The ice melted into water."
        │       • "The food turned into poison."
        │       • "He cried himself into a frenzy." (Reflexive fake object CTV frame).
        │       💡 The "Into" Transition Rule: When the result marks a radical material 
        │          transformation, phase change, or fragmentation, English selects a PP 
        │          headed by "into". Because the verb frame requires this transition 
        │          to mark culmination, the PP is an obligatory Complement (not an adjunct).
        │
        └── Resultative Adjuncts (Optional Endpoints on Saturated Events)
            ├── With Unaccusative Verbs (Adjective Realization):
            │   • "The river froze solid." ("The river froze" is complete; 'solid' adds an endpoint).
            │   • "The lake water froze thick."
            │
            └── With Monotransitive Verbs (Optional Prepositional Endpoints):
                • "He wiped the table to a shine." ("He wiped the table" is a complete event; 
                  'to a shine' optionally specifies the degree of polish).
```

## 3. Secondary Predication Diagnostics: AP vs. PP Endpoints

A common point of confusion is distinguishing direct adjective results from prepositional path results.

### Diagnostic Case Study: "He wiped the table shiny" vs. "He wiped the table to a shine"

1. **Direct Predication vs. Circumstantial Path:**
   * In *"He wiped the table **shiny**"*, the adjective phrase ($\text{AP}$) directly predicates an internal physical state onto the direct object ($[\text{the table} \rightarrow \text{shiny}]$). This direct state change shifts the verb into a **Complex Transitive Verb (CTV)** frame ($\text{Subject} + \text{CTV} + \text{DO} + \text{OC}$), making *shiny* an obligatory **Resultative Object Complement**.
   * In *"He wiped the table **to a shine**"*, the preposition *to* introduces a circumstantial degree path (*to the point of producing a shine*). The core predicate remains a saturated **Monotransitive Verb** (*"He wiped the table"* is already structurally complete), making *"to a shine"* an optional **Resultative Adjunct**.

2. **The Deletion Diagnostic:**
   * Dropping an obligatory complement collapses the intended causative predication frame or alters the subcategorization requirement.
   * Dropping an adjunct leaves the primary clausal proposition fully grammatical and semantically intact.

| Sentence Construction                | Constituent Category                   | Syntactic Status        | Syntactic Function                       | Semantic Mechanism                                           |
| :----------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :---------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- |
| *He wiped the table **shiny**.*      | **Adjective Phrase ($\text{AP}$)**     | Obligatory in CTV frame | **Resultative Object Complement**        | Small-clause result directly characterizing the direct object ($DO \rightarrow \text{State}$). |
| *He wiped the table **to a shine**.* | **Prepositional Phrase ($\text{PP}$)** | Optional                | **Resultative Adjunct**                  | Modifies a saturated monotransitive event with an optional degree path. |
| *The vase broke **into pieces**.*    | **Prepositional Phrase ($\text{PP}$)** | Obligatory              | **Prepositional Resultative Complement** | Radical physical fragmentation requires a mandatory transformational boundary. |
| *The river froze **solid**.*         | **Adjective Phrase ($\text{AP}$)**     | Optional                | **Resultative Adjunct**                  | Optional terminal bound adjoined to an unaccusative verb.    |
| *She considers him **brilliant**.*   | **Adjective Phrase ($\text{AP}$)**     | Obligatory in CTV frame | **Evaluative Depictive Complement**      | Stative mental evaluation characterizing the object concurrent with the event. |

## 4. Analytical Guardrails & Boundary Diagnostics

### Guardrail 1: Depictive Adjective vs. Manner Adverb
Do not confuse a depictive adjective (which predicates a concurrent state of a noun entity) with a manner adverb (which modifies the mechanical execution of the verb):
* *The engineer returned to the room **calm**.* $\rightarrow$ **Subject Depictive Adjunct** (Describes the psychological state of the engineer during the return).
* *The engineer returned to the room **calmly**.* $\rightarrow$ **Manner Adverbial** (Describes the physical manner/pace of walking back).
* *The automated process terminated **silent**.* ❌ (Crash: *silent* cannot characterize an abstract event process).
* *The automated process terminated **silently**.* ✅ (**Manner Adverbial** modifying verb termination).

### Guardrail 2: Attributive-Only vs. Predicate-Only Adjectives
Certain adjectives carry rigid distributional restrictions:
* **Attributive-Only Adjectives:** Cannot appear in predicate positions (*utter, chief, mere, sheer*).
  * ✅ *An **utter** failure.*
  * ❌ *\*The failure was utter.*
* **Predicate-Only Adjectives ($a$-adjectives):** Reject pre-nominal attributive slots (*afraid, asleep, alive, alone, aware*).
  * ✅ *The operator was **asleep**.*
  * ❌ *\*An asleep operator missed the alarm.* (Must shift to attributive participle: *A sleeping operator...*).
