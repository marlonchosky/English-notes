# Interrogative Words and Verb Agreement

When interrogative pronouns (*who*, *which*, *what*, *whose*) serve as the grammatical subject of a clause, their grammatical number feature ($[\pm \text{Singular}]$) is not permanently fixed. Instead, verb agreement is determined by **Interrogative Selection (Head Primacy)**, **Answer-Set Number Feature Inheritance**, or the **Default Singular Strategy**.

---

## 1. Core Syntactic Principle: Variable-Number Operators

Unlike central pronouns with invariant phi-features (*he* $[+\text{Sg}]$, *they* $[+\text{Pl}]$), interrogative $wh$-pronouns are variable-number operators. 

Their agreement feature is valued at the interface between syntax and semantics through one of three hierarchical mechanisms:
1. **The Target Selection Strategy (Expected Answer Set):** The verb copies the number feature anticipated by the speaker.
2. **The Default Singular Strategy:** When the target set is non-restricted, unknown, or open-ended, syntax defaults to 3rd-person singular $[+\text{Sg}]$.
3. **The Binary Comparison Constraint ($N = 2$):** When selecting between two options, singular agreement is syntactically mandatory.

---

## 2. Agreement Strategies in Subject Wh-Questions

### A. The Default Singular Strategy (Unknown / Open Target)
When the question does not specify a plural count, English defaults to singular verb agreement:
* *"**Who is** responsible for maintaining this CI/CD pipeline?"*
  * Evaluates an unknown target set; defaults to singular (*is*).
* *"**Which of the candidates has** submitted the assignment?"*
  * Evaluates individual candidate selection; defaults to singular (*has*).
* *"**What causes** these intermittent latency spikes?"*
  * Target root cause is unspecified; defaults to singular (*causes*).

### B. Expected Plural Answer Set
When the interrogative operator explicitly targets multiple entities or plural outputs, the $wh$-word inherits $[+\text{Plural}]$ and selects a plural verb:
* *"**Which of the database nodes are** failing health checks right now?"*
  * The speaker explicitly queries for multiple failing nodes ($N > 1$).
* *"**Who are** the developers assigned to this ticket?"*
  * Plural predication in the complement (*developers*) forces plural agreement on the operator.
* *"**What are** the primary causes of this system latency?"*
  * The complement (*causes*) specifies an anticipated plural answer set.

### C. The Binary Comparison Constraint ($N = 2$)
When comparing two entities to evaluate which one satisfies a predicate, the domain is strictly restricted to $N = 2$. Because only one option can be selected as superior, the construction **strictly enforces singular verb agreement**:
* ✅ *"**Which of these two deployment scripts runs** faster?"*
* ❌ *\*"Which of these two deployment scripts run faster?"* (Agreement crash: $N = 2$ requires singular)
* ✅ *"**Which of the two caching algorithms reduces** latency better?"*
* ✅ *"**Which of these two frameworks supports** asynchronous handlers?"*

## 3. Interrogative Selection (Head Primacy) vs. Partitive Look-Through

A critical boundary in syntax is distinguishing between the **Look-Through Mechanism** of transparent quantifiers and the **Head Primacy** of interrogative pronouns:

```text
Agreement Mechanisms Compared
├── A. Transparent Partitive Quantifiers (Look-Through):
│      Verb looks through the quantifier and agrees with the object of 'of'.
│      • Some of the microservices [Plural] ARE failing.
│      • Most of the legacy code [Mass] WAS refactored.
│
└── B. Interrogative Pronoun Partitives (Head Primacy):
       Verb agreement is governed by the selective operator scope (1 vs. many).
       • Which [Singular Head Selection] of the microservices IS failing?
       • Who [Singular Individual Scope] of the engineers KNOWS C#?
```

| Construction Type                                       | Structural Mechanism                                         | Example Sentence                                  | Verb Agreement                       |
| :------------------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------------- |
| **Transparent Quantifier** (*some of, most of, all of*) | **Look-Through:** Agrees with the nominal inside the $PP$.   | *"**Some of the microservices have** crashed."*   | Plural (governed by *microservices*) |
| **Transparent Quantifier** (*most of, all of*)          | **Look-Through:** Agrees with the mass noun inside the $PP$. | *"**Most of the legacy code was** refactored."*   | Singular (governed by *code*)        |
| **Interrogative Selection** (*Which of...*)             | **Head Primacy:** Focuses on an individual selected unit.    | *"**Which of the microservices is** failing?"*    | Singular (selective focus)           |
| **Interrogative Selection** (*Who of...*)               | **Singular Scope:** Targets an individual candidate.         | *"**Who of the remaining developers knows** C#?"* | Singular (candidate search)          |

> 💡 **Notional Variation in Casual Registers:** While formal generative syntax enforces singular head primacy for *which of the [plural noun]* (*"Which of the servers was restarted?"*), plural agreement (*"Which of the servers were restarted?"*) is accepted when the speaker explicitly queries for multiple units ($N > 1$).

## 4. Agreement Mechanics with "Whose"

Agreement with *whose* depends directly on whether it projects as an **Interrogative Determiner ($D^\circ$)** or an **Interrogative Pronoun ($NP$ Head)**:

### A. Determiner Frame ($D^\circ$ + Overt Noun)
When *whose* acts as a determiner, it carries no independent number feature. The finite verb agrees with the **overt head noun** of the subject $DP$:
* *"**Whose log files were** archived yesterday?"*
  * *whose* ($D^\circ$) modifies plural head *files* $\longrightarrow$ Plural agreement (*were*).
* *"**Whose pull request was** merged into main?"*
  * *whose* ($D^\circ$) modifies singular head *pull request* $\longrightarrow$ Singular agreement (*was*).

### B. Pronoun Frame (Predicate Inversion: Whose + Verb + Subject)
When *whose* stands alone as a predicate pronoun, the verb does **not** agree with *whose*; it agrees with the **post-verbal subject DP**:
* *"We found an access key. **Whose is this key**?"*
  * Subject = *this key* $[+\text{Sg}]$ $\longrightarrow$ Singular verb (*is*).
* *"We found two disconnected servers. **Whose are these servers**?"*
  * Subject = *these servers* $[+\text{Pl}]$ $\longrightarrow$ Plural verb (*are*).
